Rally · the done state

What this looks like when it's finished

One laptop. One browser tab. One npm run dev. A dark display on the room TV, the Ambiguous workspace beside it, and an agent sitting in the meeting with a microphone and hands.

This document describes the finished artifact — every screen region, every mechanism, and every path a spoken sentence takes on its way to becoming a real object in a real workspace. It is the target that PLAN.md is the schedule for.

1 · The thing itself

Rally is a voice agent that joins a meeting the way a quiet colleague does: it sits there, it follows along, and it does not speak. The microphone is open the entire time and the transcript scrolls continuously on the room display, so everyone can see it is paying attention — but it produces no audio, no summaries, no suggestions, no interruptions.

When someone says its name, it acts inside the conversation. Not afterwards, not in a digest email, not in a document nobody opens. Within about five seconds of "Rally, file that" there is a real task in a real workspace, with an owner and a priority, and Rally has said so out loud.

The finished product is two screens side by side, and the second one is the point. The left screen is Rally. The right screen is the Ambiguous workspace — the actual team workspace, with actual Tasks, Mail, Calendar, Docs and Chat — filling up in real time as Rally works. Every object it creates is attributed to a workspace member named Rally, with its own identity and its own avatar. Not a bot account posting on someone's behalf. A teammate.

The one-sentence version. Every meeting assistant on the market produces a transcript you read later. Rally produces work, during the meeting, and it is the only one whose headline feature is the discipline to stay silent until asked.

2 · The room display

This is what's on the TV, and what fills most of the demo video. It is designed to be read from across a room by someone who is not looking directly at it: dark ground, few elements, one large moving thing.

The orb carries the pitch

It's the only element readable from across a room, and it tells the whole story without words: dim and slowly breathing for minutes on end, then snapping to full brightness the instant someone says the name. Dim is the state that's on screen longest, so it's the state that gets the most design attention.

The transcript proves the silence

Without a visible transcript, "it's listening" is a claim. With one scrolling past while the orb stays dim, it's evidence. Wake lines get an accent bar so the moment reads on video.

Rally's speech is rendered, not just heard

Everything Rally says also appears as a bordered block in the transcript. Room audio is unreliable on a phone recording; the caption is not.

The feed proves the action

A card slides in from the right the instant a tool returns, colour-coded by which Ambiguous app it landed in, chipped with owner and priority, and linked to the real object.

3 · The wake gate

This is the mechanism the whole product rests on, and it is worth being precise about because the naive version does not work. The naive version is to connect a Realtime session and write "only respond when addressed as Rally" in the system prompt. That fails. The model will interrupt, it will interrupt unpredictably, and it will interrupt during the demo.

The real implementation never gives the model the option. The session is configured to segment turns and transcribe them, but with response creation switched off at the session level. The model physically cannot produce audio on its own. The only code path that leads to speech is a manual response.create, and that line only executes after a string match.

4 · How a sentence becomes an object

End to end, from a person's mouth to a row in a workspace and back out of a speaker. The browser holds the voice session directly over WebRTC, so there is no server-side audio plumbing anywhere in this path — the server only ever sees JSON.

5 · Two brains

A Realtime session is a WebRTC connection with a sub-second turn budget that dies when the tab closes. It is the right tool for hearing and speaking and the wrong tool for anything that takes four minutes or needs to exist tomorrow morning. So Rally has a second, slower half.

Why this matters more than it sounds. Every other beat in the demo is a fast voice agent doing a fast thing. This one is the product continuing to work after the conversation that created it ended — which is the difference between a voice interface and an actual agent, and it is the single thing the organizers most consistently reward.

10 · Definition of done

Not "the code compiles." These are the seven things you must be able to demonstrate on demand, in front of someone, without a rehearsal:

  • It stays quiet. Three people hold a real sixty-second conversation and Rally makes no sound — and you can point at the code path that makes interruption structurally impossible rather than merely unlikely.
  • It acts inside the conversation. One spoken sentence produces a real task in a real workspace in under five seconds, and Rally confirms out loud without being asked to.
  • It is a teammate, not a bot. Every artifact in Ambiguous reads created by Rally, and every card on the feed deep-links to the real object.
  • It compounds. One utterance fires three tools — calendar, mail, docs — without stopping to ask which to do first.
  • It remembers. It answers a question about a meeting that happened last week, from storage that survived a process restart.
  • It keeps working after you stop talking. A delegated task returns on its own, into Ambiguous Chat, while the demo is still running.
  • It survives the room. Four independent fallbacks: AMBIGUOUS_MODE=mock, HERMES_MODE=canned, ?sim=1, and a clean recording made by 17:00.

11 · What is honestly held together with tape

This is a one-day build and the README says so out loud, because judges at this event explicitly reward "here's what broke" over a silently faked demo.

  • No speaker diarization. Every transcript line is attributed to "Room" unless a human labels it. Real diarization is a day of work for a cosmetic gain.
  • Recall is LIKE '%term%' over about fifty rows, not a vector search. On camera the two are indistinguishable and one costs four minutes instead of forty.
  • Dates are parsed by chrono-node, not reasoned about. "Tomorrow at 3" works; "the Tuesday after the offsite" does not.
  • Attendees are hardcoded in /api/context. There is no calendar integration deciding who is in the room.
  • No auth, no multi-user, no tests, no deployment. It runs on one laptop at localhost:3000 and that is the intended production environment for exactly one day.
  • The memory table is seeded with three facts from a fictional previous meeting, so the recall beat has something to recall.

The parts that are genuinely real: the wake gate, the Realtime voice loop, the Ambiguous writes, and the Hermes hand-off. Those four are the product. Everything above is scaffolding around them, and saying so is worth more than pretending otherwise.
